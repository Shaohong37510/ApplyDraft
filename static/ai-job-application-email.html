The Problem With Generic AI-Generated Emails

Hiring managers now read hundreds of AI-written cover letters and emails every week. They recognize them in the first sentence — and it does not help your application.

When you open ChatGPT and type "write me a job application email for a design position," what you get is statistically the same email that thousands of other candidates are sending to the same firms on the same day. The AI has no knowledge of your specific background. It does not know which internships you completed, which firms shaped your professional perspective, which specific project in the job description connects directly to your real experience, or what makes your particular path genuinely interesting to this particular employer. It fills every one of those gaps with generic filler phrases.

The phrases that result are immediately recognizable to anyone who reads applications professionally. Every senior hiring manager, every creative director, every architecture principal reading applications in 2025 and 2026 has seen these sentences hundreds of times:

"I am writing to express my sincere interest in this exciting opportunity." "I am a passionate and dedicated professional who would be a great fit for your team." "I am eager to bring my unique skill set to your organization." "I am excited to contribute to your team's continued success." These sentences signal one thing clearly and unambiguously: the writer did not spend time on this specific application.

The irony is that using AI carelessly can actually make you look worse than writing nothing at all. A hiring manager who reads a formulaic, generic AI-generated email does not think "this person is efficient and modern." They think "this person does not care enough about our firm to write something real." That impression is a harder hole to climb out of than a simple, brief, genuine email written in your own imperfect voice.

The solution is not to stop using AI. It is to use AI that is grounded in your actual background, your verifiable experience, and the specific details of the firm and role you are targeting. That is a fundamentally different kind of tool — and it is exactly what separates applications that earn interviews from applications that earn silence.

The core problem with generic AI: It generates from statistical averages. It knows what architecture cover letters "typically" sound like and produces more of the same. Good AI application tools generate from your specific data — your actual CV, your real internship history, your genuine writing style — and apply that data to the specific firm you are targeting. The output is different because the input is different.

What AI Should and Shouldn't Do in Your Application

AI is a drafting tool. Like any tool, whether it helps or hurts you depends entirely on how you use it — and what you give it to work from.

AI Should

  • Draw exclusively from your actual uploaded CV and experience — nothing invented
  • Tailor each email to the specific firm and the specific job description
  • Mirror your natural tone and communication style from sample writing
  • Reference specific, verifiable facts about the target firm
  • Keep the email concise — under 200 words in the body
  • Generate a genuinely unique first draft for each firm, not a template swap
  • Save to your drafts for review before anything is sent
  • Flag when the job description is too vague to generate specific content

AI Should Not

  • Invent internships, projects, skills, or credentials you do not have
  • Use generic phrases that immediately signal AI authorship
  • Adopt an overly casual or overly formal tone without matching your style
  • Reference outdated or unverifiable information about the firm
  • Make performance promises you cannot keep
  • Send anything without your explicit review and approval
  • Replace the personal judgment and knowledge only you can bring
  • Produce identical emails for every firm with only the name changed

The Email and Cover Letter Combination

Sending both a well-crafted email and a professionally formatted cover letter PDF significantly increases your chances — but only if you use each format correctly and for its intended purpose.

The email and the cover letter serve fundamentally different purposes and should not overlap significantly in content. The email is your first contact — it is brief, personal, and specifically designed to make the hiring manager want to open the attachment. Think of it as the pitch: its entire job is to earn the click. It should be under 200 words, reference something specific and verifiable about the firm, and end with a clear, low-pressure ask.

The cover letter, attached as a PDF, is your professional case statement. It provides the fuller picture of your background, expands on one or two experiences that are most directly relevant to this specific role, and demonstrates that you can write clearly, think carefully, and present yourself professionally in a formal document format. A well-designed, well-formatted cover letter PDF also signals attention to visual presentation — which is particularly important in architecture, design, and creative fields where how you present information is itself a signal of professional competence.

The most common mistake candidates make is copying their entire cover letter into the email body. This produces an email that is far too long, feels impersonal and templated, and wastes the opportunity that the email format uniquely provides. Equally, sending a cover letter PDF with a single-sentence email ("Please find my application attached") throws away the chance to create a personal connection before the attachment is ever opened — and a one-line email often does not earn the click to open anything at all.

The right format: A brief, personalized email body of 150–200 words that hooks the reader, establishes a specific connection to their work, and creates genuine context for your attached materials. Then a professionally formatted, one-page PDF cover letter that makes the full professional case. Both generated per-firm, both grounded in your actual background, both reviewed and sent by you.

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions people actually ask before trusting an AI tool with their professional job applications — answered honestly.

Will hiring managers know the email was written by AI?
Not if the AI is working from the right inputs. The red flag hiring managers are trained to notice is not "AI-assisted" — it is "generic." An email that references the firm's specific recent projects by name, draws from a candidate's verifiable real experience, and is written in a consistent natural voice will read as genuine regardless of whether AI was used to draft it. The standard is whether the email accurately represents you and your interest in this specific firm — not whether you wrote every word from a blank page.
Does AI ever make up experience I don't actually have?
Generic AI tools like ChatGPT can and do embellish or invent experience when they lack sufficient real information to work from — they fill the gap with plausible-sounding claims that are not verifiable. This is one of the core reasons ApplyDraft requires your actual CV and cover letter samples as source inputs. Every claim in an ApplyDraft draft should be directly traceable to something in your uploaded documents. If you notice anything in a draft that overstates your background, edit it before sending — that review step is a built-in part of the workflow for exactly this reason.
Can I edit the AI draft before I send it?
Yes — and you always should. ApplyDraft saves drafts to your Gmail or Outlook drafts folder, never to your sent mail. Reviewing, adjusting, and personalizing the draft before sending is an expected and encouraged part of the workflow. You might want to adjust a phrase that does not quite capture your voice, add a specific detail from the job posting the AI did not emphasize, or tighten the closing line. The AI provides a strong, personalized first draft; your judgment and knowledge of the situation bring it to final quality.
Does ApplyDraft work for all industries?
ApplyDraft works best for knowledge-work roles where written communication and professional presentation are part of the expected application process — architecture, design, engineering, consulting, creative fields, academic and research roles, journalism, and adjacent areas. It is less suited to positions where the application process is entirely resume-driven with no email component, or for highly standardized technical roles at large corporations where the hiring process is fully controlled by HR portals with no direct contact pathway.
How is ApplyDraft different from just using ChatGPT?
The key differences are inputs, context, and integration. ChatGPT starts from a blank slate every session — you have to paste your background, the job description, instructions about tone, and guidance about the firm every single time, and carefully evaluate the output for accuracy and appropriateness. What if the job description is vague or minimal?
Vague job descriptions are common — especially at smaller firms that post a brief one-paragraph listing without detailed requirements. When the posting provides limited specific information to work from, ApplyDraft shifts weight to firm-level research: recent completed projects, stated design philosophy, published interviews, team composition, and observable culture signals from the firm's own website and portfolio.
