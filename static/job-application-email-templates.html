How to Customize These Templates

A template used without customization will read like a template. These five steps convert a starting point into a compelling, personal email.

1

Replace Every Bracket Placeholder

Search for every instance of text in brackets and replace it with real, specific information. This includes your name, the firm name, the position title, your university, and any skill placeholders. If you can't fill in a placeholder, the template is telling you something you need to research before sending. Never send an email with "[FIRM NAME]" or "[YOUR NAME]" still in the body — it happens more often than you'd think.

2

Add One Specific Detail About Their Work

Every template above has a slot for a specific project or initiative you've observed about the firm. This is not optional filler — it's the most important personalization you'll make. Spend ten minutes on their website and find one project, publication, or stated value that genuinely resonates with you. Name it specifically. "Your recent renovation of [Building Name]" or "Your essay on adaptive reuse in the 2025 issue of [Publication]" shows you did the work, which sets you apart from 90% of applicants.

3

Match the Tone to the Firm's Culture

A boutique design studio founded by two architects in Brooklyn reads email differently than the human resources department of a global engineering firm. Look at the firm's website copy, social media tone, and any published writing to gauge their voice. Adjust accordingly: more formal for traditional or corporate contexts, slightly warmer for studio environments. The templates above skew professional — you can loosen the language for creative firms without changing the structure.

4

Choose Your Strongest Accomplishment

Each template has a slot for a relevant accomplishment. Don't default to the most recent thing you did — choose the most relevant thing. If the firm works on residential adaptive reuse and you have one project in that area among many others, lead with that one. The goal is for the hiring manager to finish reading your email and think, "This person has done something directly relevant to what we do." One specific, relevant accomplishment beats three vague ones every time.

5

Read It Out Loud Before Sending

After you've filled in all the placeholders and made your adjustments, read the full email out loud from the subject line to the signature. Listen for awkward transitions between your additions and the template language — these are the seams that reveal a copied-and-pasted draft. Smooth them out. Check that the flow is natural, that no sentence sounds robotic, and that the email sounds like one continuous, purposeful message rather than assembled parts.

What to Attach (and How to Name Your Files)

Your attachments are what get you the interview. Make sure they're the right files, named correctly, and in the right format.

📄

Resume (Always)

Format: PDF. File name: FirstName_LastName_Resume.pdf. Keep it to one page if you have under 5 years of experience. Your resume should be tailored to the position — not the same document you've been using for every application. Make sure it opens cleanly on different systems by exporting from your design software or Word as a print-quality PDF.

📝

Cover Letter (When Requested or When It Helps)

Format: PDF. File name: FirstName_LastName_CoverLetter.pdf. Attach when the posting requests it, or when you have enough to say that the email body can't contain it. Never paste the cover letter into the email body. Make sure it's addressed to the correct firm — a cover letter addressed to a different company is a disqualifying mistake that happens more than you'd think.

🖼️

Portfolio (For Design and Creative Roles)

Format: PDF, ideally under 15MB. File name: FirstName_LastName_Portfolio.pdf. If your portfolio PDF is large, host it online (on your personal site or a service like Issuu or Cargo) and include the link in the email body — this avoids email size limit issues. Always mention in the body that a portfolio is attached or linked. A portfolio that goes unmentioned often goes unopened.

📋

References (Only if Requested)

Format: PDF. File name: FirstName_LastName_References.pdf. Don't attach references unless the posting specifically asks for them. Most firms request references only during later stages. Attaching them unsolicited can make your email feel disorganized and adds file weight. Have a references document ready to send on request — with three professional contacts, their titles, their relationship to you, and their direct contact info.

The attachment rule: Mention every file you've attached by name in the email body. "I've attached my resume and portfolio" tells the reader what to expect and invites them to open the files. Always do a final check to confirm the files are actually attached before hitting send — this is the most common and most embarrassing mistake in job application emails.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about using and customizing job application email templates.

Will hiring managers know I used a template?
If you personalize it properly, no — and it doesn't matter. Every professional writer uses frameworks. What hiring managers notice is poor personalization: generic company references, obvious placeholder text left in, or an email that could have been written for any firm. A well-customized template that has a specific project reference, the right tone, and a genuine call to action reads as a strong professional email, full stop. The goal is never the template — it's the final product.
How much should I change from the template?
At minimum: every placeholder filled in, one specific company detail added, and one specific accomplishment included. Beyond that, change whatever doesn't sound like you. The templates are structures — the voice should be yours. If a sentence feels unnatural when you read it aloud, rewrite it. The goal is an email that is both professionally structured and distinctly personal.
Should I use a different template for each application?
Yes — match the template to your situation. If you're responding to a posted opening, use Template 1. If you're cold-reaching a firm, use Template 2. If a colleague referred you, use Template 3. Using the wrong template for your situation can create a tone mismatch — for example, Template 4's student-appropriate humility would read as strange from a seasoned professional.
Can I combine elements from multiple templates?
Yes, and this is often the right approach. If you're a student with a referral, blend Template 3's referral opening with Template 4's student-appropriate framing. If you're doing cold outreach for a creative role, take Template 2's structure and add Template 5's portfolio emphasis. The templates are modular — mix and match what's most appropriate for your specific situation.
How long should the final email be?
The email body — not counting subject line, greeting, or signature — should be 200–300 words. The templates above are written to land around that range when filled in. If your personalization is pushing you past 350 words, cut rather than expand. Your attached cover letter and portfolio carry the full narrative — the email's job is to get them opened, not to replace them.
